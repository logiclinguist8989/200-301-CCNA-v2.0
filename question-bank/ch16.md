### Q1 · Drag and drop — match
<sub>Source: 16.2</sub>

Drag each 802.11 term to its description.

| Description | Term |
| --- | --- |
| The network name that users see, up to 32 characters long | SSID |
| The MAC address of one AP radio | BSSID |
| One AP radio and the clients associated to it | BSS |
| Several APs advertising the same SSID on the same network, so that clients can roam between them | ESS |
| Clients talking directly to each other with no AP | IBSS |

**Extra targets:** Beacon · Association

> **Explanation:** The **SSID** is the name shared by every AP in an ESS, while the **BSSID** is unique to each AP radio and identifies its **BSS**. An **ESS** is many BSSs with one SSID, which is what lets clients roam. An **IBSS** is an ad hoc network. A beacon is the frame an AP broadcasts about 10 times a second to advertise its SSIDs, and association is a client joining a BSS after authentication — neither matches a description.

### Q2 · Multiple choice — choose two
<sub>Source: 16.2</sub>

Which two statements about 802.11 WLANs are correct? (Choose two.)

- [x] 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) operates only in the 5 GHz band.
- [x] 802.11ax is Wi-Fi 6, and Wi-Fi 6E adds the 6 GHz band.
- [ ] 802.11g operates in the 5 GHz band at up to 54 Mb/s.
- [ ] 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) operates only in the 2.4 GHz band.
- [ ] Wi-Fi is full duplex, so an AP can send and receive on the same channel at the same time.

> **Explanation:** 802.11ac is a 5 GHz-only standard, and 802.11ax works in 2.4 and 5 GHz, with Wi-Fi 6E adding 6 GHz. 802.11g is a 2.4 GHz standard; 54 Mb/s in 5 GHz describes 802.11a. 802.11n works in both 2.4 and 5 GHz. Wi-Fi is half duplex and shared: only one station transmits on a channel at a time, which is why 802.11 uses CSMA/CA.

### Q3 · Multiple choice
<sub>Source: 16.3, 16.6</sub>

Refer to the exhibit.

```
$ nmcli -f IN-USE,BSSID,SSID,CHAN,SIGNAL,SECURITY device wifi list
IN-USE  BSSID              SSID        CHAN  SIGNAL  SECURITY
*       70:7D:B9:AA:BB:22  Staff-WiFi  36    86      WPA2 802.1X
        70:7D:B9:AA:BB:21  Staff-WiFi  1     81      WPA2 802.1X
        70:7D:B9:AA:BB:31  Staff-WiFi  3     74      WPA2 802.1X
        70:7D:B9:AA:BB:41  Staff-WiFi  6     68      WPA2 802.1X
        70:7D:B9:AA:BB:51  Staff-WiFi  11    47      WPA2 802.1X
```

An engineer runs this scan from a Linux laptop on the second floor, where users on 2.4 GHz report corrupted frames and many retries. Which AP radio is causing adjacent-channel interference?

- [ ] 70:7D:B9:AA:BB:22 on channel 36
- [ ] 70:7D:B9:AA:BB:21 on channel 1
- [x] 70:7D:B9:AA:BB:31 on channel 3
- [ ] 70:7D:B9:AA:BB:51 on channel 11

> **Explanation:** 2.4 GHz channels are only 5 MHz apart but about 22 MHz wide, so only 1, 6 and 11 do not overlap. Channel 3 overlaps both channel 1 and channel 6, and overlapping channels corrupt each other's frames — the retries the users see. Move that radio onto 1, 6 or 11 so that it differs from its neighbours, or, in a dense area, turn that 2.4 GHz radio off. Channel 36 is in the 5 GHz band and cannot overlap a 2.4 GHz channel, and channels 1 and 11 are part of the non-overlapping set. A weak signal on channel 11 is not interference.

### Q4 · Multiple choice
<sub>Source: 16.3, 16.6</sub>

After a refurbishment, an engineer set every AP radio to maximum transmit power to improve coverage. Users now report lower throughput, and laptops stay connected to a distant AP after walking past a closer one. What is the best fix?

- [x] Lower the transmit power so that neighbouring cells overlap by roughly 15–20 %, adding APs where coverage becomes too thin
- [ ] Put every AP on the same channel so that clients can roam without changing frequency
- [ ] Bond the 2.4 GHz channels to 40 MHz to give each client more capacity
- [ ] Hide the SSID so that clients stop associating with distant APs

> **Explanation:** Too much transmit power makes large, heavily overlapping cells: more co-channel interference and "sticky" clients that stay on a far AP. The remedy is lower power and, where needed, more APs, with about 15–20 % cell overlap for roaming. Putting every AP on one channel creates co-channel interference, so they all take turns. 40 MHz in 2.4 GHz leaves only one usable non-overlapping channel, so 2.4 GHz should stay at 20 MHz. Hiding the SSID does not change cell size or roaming, and it is not a security measure either.

### Q5 · Multiple choice
<sub>Source: 16.4</sub>

An AP in a warehouse feeds a directional patch antenna with a gain of 9 dBi. The radio transmits at 14 dBm, and the antenna cable has a loss of 3 dB. What is the EIRP?

- [x] 20 dBm (100 mW)
- [ ] 26 dBm (400 mW)
- [ ] 14 dBm (25 mW)
- [ ] 11 dBm (12.5 mW)

> **Explanation:** EIRP = transmitter power + antenna gain − cable loss = 14 + 9 − 3 = **20 dBm**. From 0 dBm = 1 mW, each +10 dB multiplies by ten, so 20 dBm = 100 mW. 26 dBm (400 mW) adds the cable loss instead of subtracting it. 14 dBm is the transmitter power alone, but regulators limit EIRP — the power that leaves the antenna. 11 dBm ignores the antenna gain.

### Q6 · Drag and drop — match
<sub>Source: 16.4</sub>

Drag the RF behaviour to the situation that it describes.

| Situation | RF behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| A concrete wall and a room full of people weaken the signal by soaking up its energy | Absorption |
| The signal bounces off metal cabinets and a lift shaft | Reflection |
| The signal bends as it passes from air into glass | Refraction |
| Foliage and rough surfaces spread the signal in many directions | Scattering |
| The signal bends around a pillar, leaving an RF shadow behind it | Diffraction |
| Several copies of the same signal reach the receiver by different paths at slightly different times | Multipath |

**Extra targets:** Free-space path loss

> **Explanation:** Absorption is energy soaked up by materials such as water, people and concrete. Reflection is a bounce off smooth surfaces, refraction is bending between materials, scattering spreads the signal in many directions, and diffraction bends it around an obstacle. Multipath is usually caused by reflections indoors; MIMO turns it into an advantage, while older radios suffer from it. Free-space path loss is the weakening of the signal with distance alone, which is why 5 GHz cells are smaller than 2.4 GHz cells.

### Q7 · Drag and drop — match
<sub>Source: 16.3, 16.5</sub>

Drag the wireless security option to each description. An option can be used more than once.

| Description | Security option |
| --- | --- |
| RC4 with a static key; cracked in minutes | WEP |
| TKIP, an interim fix that is now deprecated | WPA |
| AES-CCMP, with a pre-shared key (PSK) in Personal mode | WPA2 |
| SAE replaces the pre-shared key, and protected management frames are mandatory | WPA3 |
| Required on the 6 GHz band | WPA3 |
| Encrypts an open guest network without a password | OWE (Enhanced Open) |

**Extra targets:** Open

> **Explanation:** WEP (1999) is broken and must never be used. WPA (2003) added RC4-based TKIP as a stopgap and is deprecated. WPA2 (2004) uses AES-CCMP and is secure with a strong passphrase. WPA3 (2018) replaces PSK with SAE, makes PMF mandatory, adds GCMP-256 in its 192-bit Enterprise mode and is required on 6 GHz. OWE (Enhanced Open) encrypts a guest network that has no password. A plain open network has no encryption at all, so anyone can read its data.

### Q8 · Drag and drop — order
<sub>Source: 16.5</sub>

A laptop joins a WPA2-Enterprise WLAN. Drag the steps into the order in which they happen.

1. The laptop completes 802.11 open authentication and associates to the AP
2. The AP sends an EAP identity request, and the laptop replies with its identity
3. The laptop and the RADIUS server run the EAP method, such as a TLS tunnel with credentials, relayed by the AP
4. The RADIUS server sends an Access-Accept to the AP
5. The laptop and the AP complete the four-way handshake to create session keys
6. Encrypted user traffic starts to flow

> **Explanation:** In 802.1X the laptop's software is the **supplicant**, the AP (or the WLC) is the **authenticator**, and RADIUS is the **authentication server**. The authenticator only relays EAP between the other two until the RADIUS Access-Accept, which is the decision point. The AP then sends EAP-Success, and the four-way handshake creates the session keys; only after that does encrypted user traffic start.

### Q9 · Multiple choice — choose two
<sub>Source: 16.2, 16.5</sub>

Refer to the exhibit.

```
C:\> netsh wlan show interfaces

    Name                   : Wi-Fi
    State                  : connected
    SSID                   : Warehouse-Scan
    BSSID                  : 70:7d:b9:aa:bb:61
    Radio type             : 802.11g
    Authentication         : WPA-Personal
    Cipher                 : TKIP
    Band                   : 2.4 GHz
    Channel                : 11
    Receive rate (Mbps)    : 54
    Transmit rate (Mbps)   : 54
    Signal                 : 76%
    Profile                : Warehouse-Scan
```

An auditor checks how a handheld scanner connects to the warehouse WLAN. Which two conclusions are correct? (Choose two.)

- [x] The WLAN uses WPA with RC4-based TKIP, a deprecated interim fix, and should move to WPA2 with AES-CCMP or to WPA3.
- [x] Every scanner uses the same passphrase, so when an employee who knows it leaves, it must be changed on every device.
- [ ] Each scanner authenticates to a RADIUS server with its own credentials.
- [ ] The traffic is encrypted with AES-CCMP, the same cipher that WPA2 uses.
- [ ] The scanner is connected to a 5 GHz radio, as 802.11g requires.

> **Explanation:** `WPA-Personal` with `TKIP` is the 2003 interim protocol, now deprecated; WPA2 would show the CCMP (AES) cipher. *Personal* means one shared passphrase checked by the AP itself, so removing one person's access means changing the passphrase everywhere. With Enterprise mode, each device has its own credentials checked by RADIUS through 802.1X, and IT disables one account instead. 802.11g is a 2.4 GHz standard with a maximum of 54 Mb/s, which matches the band, channel 11 and rates shown.

### Q10 · Multiple choice
<sub>Source: 16.4</sub>

What is the signal-to-noise ratio of this connection, and how should it be judged?

- [x] 14 dB — unreliable, even though the signal strength is good
- [ ] 14 dB — good, because the RSSI is better than −67 dBm
- [ ] 130 dB — excellent
- [ ] −14 dB — the noise is stronger than the signal

> **Explanation:** SNR = signal − noise = −58 − (−72) = **14 dB**. An SNR below 15 dB is unreliable; 25 dB or more is good. The RSSI of −58 dBm is strong, which is why the bars look full — but bars show RSSI, not SNR. 130 dB comes from adding the two numbers without their signs. A negative SNR would mean the noise is stronger than the signal, but −58 dBm is stronger than −72 dBm.

### Q11 · Multiple choice
<sub>Source: 16.4, 16.6</sub>

What is the most likely cause of the problem?

- [x] A non-Wi-Fi device in the 2.4 GHz band near the user, such as a cordless phone or a wireless camera, has raised the noise floor
- [ ] The user is too far from the AP, so the received signal is too weak
- [ ] Radar has forced the AP to leave a DFS channel
- [ ] Another AP on channel 6 is causing co-channel interference, which corrupts frames

> **Explanation:** A noise floor is often about −90 to −95 dBm; −72 dBm means something is adding RF energy on the channel. Non-Wi-Fi devices in 2.4 GHz (microwave ovens, Bluetooth, cordless phones, baby monitors, wireless cameras) raise the noise floor and lower the SNR, and the colleagues on 5 GHz are unaffected. The RSSI of −58 dBm rules out distance. DFS and radar apply only to some 5 GHz channels, and the client is on 2.4 GHz channel 6. Co-channel interference makes APs and clients take turns, which lowers capacity, but it does not corrupt frames.

### Q12 · Multiple choice — choose two
<sub>Source: 16.3, 16.6</sub>

Which two actions should the engineer take? (Choose two.)

- [x] Use a spectrum analyzer, or Cisco CleanAir on the AP, to identify and locate the non-Wi-Fi source
- [x] Have the user's laptop connect on the 5 GHz band instead of 2.4 GHz
- [ ] Raise the AP's transmit power to maximum so that the signal drowns out the noise
- [ ] Move the AP's 2.4 GHz radio to channel 4 to get away from the noise
- [ ] Widen the 2.4 GHz channel to 40 MHz so that the laptop gets a higher data rate

> **Explanation:** A spectrum analyzer (Cisco CleanAir does this inside the AP) shows non-Wi-Fi sources; once the device is found it can be moved or replaced. Moving the client to 5 GHz (or 6 GHz) avoids the crowded 2.4 GHz band, which is why the colleagues on 5 GHz have no problem. More power is not better: it enlarges cells and increases co-channel interference. Channel 4 overlaps channels 1 and 6, adding adjacent-channel interference. 2.4 GHz should use 20 MHz channels only, and wider channels need a strong, clean signal anyway.
