16.2 802.11 basics

A wireless LAN (WLAN) carries Ethernet-like frames over radio. The access point (AP) is the bridge between the radio and the wired network.

Term Meaning
SSID The network name users see (up to 32 characters), for example Staff-WiFi
BSS (basic service set) One AP radio and the clients associated to it
BSSID The MAC address of that AP radio — identifies the BSS
ESS (extended service set) Several APs advertising the same SSID on the same network, so clients roam between them
IBSS (ad hoc) Clients talking directly with no AP
Association A client joining a BSS (after authentication)
Beacon Frame an AP broadcasts about 10 times a second to advertise its SSIDs and capabilities

Wi-Fi is half duplex and shared. Only one station can transmit on a channel at a time, so 802.11 uses CSMA/CA (collision avoidance): listen before sending, wait a random back-off, and expect an acknowledgment for every unicast frame. More clients and more APs on the same channel mean more waiting.

Standard Wi-Fi name Bands Maximum data rate (theoretical)
802.11b — 2.4 GHz 11 Mb/s
802.11a — 5 GHz 54 Mb/s
802.11g — 2.4 GHz 54 Mb/s
802.11n Wi-Fi 4 2.4 and 5 GHz 600 Mb/s
802.11ac Wi-Fi 5 5 GHz about 6.9 Gb/s
802.11ax Wi-Fi 6 / 6E (6E adds 6 GHz) 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz about 9.6 Gb/s
802.11be Wi-Fi 7 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz about 46 Gb/s

Real throughput is far lower — often less than half — because of the shared medium, protocol overhead and distance. Faster standards rely on wider channels, more spatial streams (MIMO: several antennas sending different data at once) and denser modulation, which need a strong, clean signal.

16.3 Bands and channels (1.5.a)

A band is a range of frequencies; a channel is a slice of a band that one BSS uses. Which channels you may use, and at what power, is set by each country's regulator — always check local rules (in Nepal, the Nepal Telecommunications Authority).

2.4 GHz 5 GHz 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E / 7)
Channels 1–13 in most countries (1–11 in the USA), 5 MHz apart, each about 20–22 MHz wide About 25 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (36–165), grouped into U-NII bands Up to 59 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels where the full band is allowed
Non-overlapping Only 1, 6 and 11 All of them at 20 MHz All of them
Range / penetration Longest, best through walls Shorter Shortest
Interference Crowded: neighbours, Bluetooth, microwave ovens Much less; some channels need DFS (avoid radar) Very little; only new devices; WPA3 required
Typical use Coverage, old and IoT devices Most enterprise traffic High-density, high-speed areas

Why 1, 6 and 11? A 2.4 GHz channel is about 22 MHz wide but channels are only 5 MHz apart, so neighbouring channel numbers overlap (Figure 16.1). Channels five apart do not overlap, which leaves three usable channels in most of the world.

Figure 16.1 draws every 2.4 GHz channel to scale on one frequency axis. The three highlighted arcs never touch each other, while each grey arc overlaps one or two of them.

Channel width. 802.11n and later can bond adjacent 20 MHz channels into 40, 80, 160 (and on Wi-Fi 7, 320) MHz channels. Wider channels carry more data but leave fewer separate channels for neighbouring APs. In 2.4 GHz use 20 MHz only; in busy 5 GHz offices 20 or 40 MHz is common; 6 GHz has room for 80 MHz and wider.

Choosing channels for several APs (Figure 16.2):

  1. Neighbouring APs use different, non-overlapping channels; reuse a channel only for APs far enough apart not to hear each other strongly.
  2. On 2.4 GHz, alternate 1, 6, 11 in a honeycomb pattern.
  3. Set transmit power so cells overlap by roughly 15–20 % for roaming — not more; louder is not better.
  4. Prefer 5 GHz (and 6 GHz) for clients; consider turning off some 2.4 GHz radios in dense areas.
  5. In a controller-based WLAN, Radio Resource Management (RRM) on the WLC chooses channels and power automatically and adapts to interference.

Figure 16.2 is the honeycomb from step 2: each colour is one channel, and wherever two cells overlap they carry different channels, so neighbours never take turns on the same frequency.

Two failures of channel planning are tested often: co-channel interference (APs on the same channel hear each other and must take turns — capacity drops, but nothing is corrupted) and adjacent-channel interference (APs on overlapping channels such as 1 and 3 corrupt each other's frames — worse).

Memory trickThe only non-overlapping 2.4 GHz channels: 1, 6 and 11.

16.4 RF characteristics (1.5.b)

Radio frequency (RF) signals are electromagnetic waves.

Term Meaning
Frequency Cycles per second, in hertz (Hz). Wi-Fi uses 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz
Wavelength Length of one cycle: about 12.5 cm at 2.4 GHz, 6 cm at 5 GHz. Shorter waves lose strength faster and pass through obstacles less well
Amplitude Signal strength
Modulation How data is encoded on the wave; denser modulation (more bits per symbol) needs a cleaner signal

Power and decibels. Wireless power is tiny and spans a huge range, so it is expressed in dBm — decibels relative to 1 milliwatt.

Rule Example
0 dBm = 1 mW —
+3 dB doubles the power, −3 dB halves it 20 dBm = 100 mW, 17 dBm = 50 mW
+10 dB is ten times the power, −10 dB a tenth 10 dBm = 10 mW, −10 dBm = 0.1 mW
Received Wi-Fi signals are negative dBm −65 dBm is stronger than −80 dBm

Signal quality measures:

Measure Meaning Typical guidance
RSSI (received signal strength) How strong the AP's signal is at the client, in dBm −67 dBm or better for voice and video; −70 dBm adequate for data; −80 dBm poor
Noise floor Background RF energy on the channel Often about −90 to −95 dBm
SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) Signal minus noise, in dB 25 dB or more good; below 15 dB unreliable

Example: RSSI −65 dBm with a noise floor of −92 dBm gives SNR = 27 dB — good. The same RSSI next to a microwave oven raising the noise to −75 dBm gives only 10 dB, and the connection struggles even though the "bars" look full.

How signals behave on the way:

Effect What happens Example
Free-space path loss Signal weakens with distance (faster at higher frequency) 5 GHz cells are smaller than 2.4 GHz cells
Absorption Material soaks up energy Water, people, concrete walls
Reflection Signal bounces off smooth surfaces Metal cabinets, glass, lift shafts
Refraction Signal bends passing between materials Air layers, glass
Scattering Signal spreads in many directions Rough surfaces, foliage, dust
Diffraction Signal bends around an obstacle, leaving an RF shadow behind it Corners, pillars
Multipath Copies arrive by different paths at slightly different times Reflections indoors; MIMO turns it into an advantage, older radios suffer

Antennas and EIRP. An omnidirectional antenna radiates in all horizontal directions (ceiling APs); a directional antenna (patch, Yagi, dish) focuses energy one way for corridors, warehouses or point-to-point bridges. Antenna gain is in dBi. The power that actually leaves the antenna is EIRP = transmitter power + antenna gain − cable loss: for example 17 dBm + 6 dBi − 2 dB = 21 dBm. Regulators limit EIRP, not just transmitter power.

16.5 Wireless security protocols (1.5.c)

Radio reaches the car park and the next building, so a WLAN must authenticate who joins and encrypt what they send.

Protocol Year Encryption Status
Open — None Guest only, with a captive portal; data visible to anyone
WEP 1999 RC4, static key Broken — cracked in minutes; never use
WPA 2003 TKIP (RC4-based) Interim fix; deprecated
WPA2 2004 AES-CCMP Still widely used; secure with a strong passphrase
WPA3 2018 AES-CCMP or GCMP-256 (192-bit Enterprise mode) Current; SAE replaces PSK; PMF (protected management frames) mandatory; required on 6 GHz

Personal versus Enterprise:

Personal Enterprise
Credentials One shared passphrase for everyone (WPA2-PSK, WPA3-SAE) Each user or device has its own credentials
Authentication Done by the AP itself 802.1X / EAP with a RADIUS server (for example Cisco ISE)
When someone leaves Change the passphrase on every device Disable one account
Typical use Home, small office, IoT Organisations

WPA3-Personal (SAE) fixes WPA2-PSK's weakness: with PSK, an attacker who captures the four-way handshake can try passwords offline; SAE ("Dragonfly") forces every guess to be an online exchange and gives forward secrecy. OWE (Enhanced Open) encrypts open guest networks without a password.

802.1X roles (Figure 16.3): the supplicant (client software), the authenticator (the AP, or the WLC in a controller-based network) and the authentication server (RADIUS). Common EAP methods: EAP-TLS (certificates on both sides — strongest), PEAP (server certificate plus the user's password inside a TLS tunnel), and Cisco's EAP-FAST. The same RADIUS server can also place the user in a VLAN, which links to AAA in Chapter 18.

Figure 16.3 runs downward in time. The green Access-Accept is the decision point; only after it and the four-way handshake does the thick blue line of encrypted user traffic start.

Not security: hiding the SSID (it still appears in client probes) and MAC filtering (MAC addresses are easily copied) only add inconvenience.

16.6 Causes of interference (1.5.d)

Cause Effect Remedy
Co-channel interference (CCI) — nearby APs on the same channel APs and clients take turns; capacity drops Better channel plan, lower power, fewer 2.4 GHz radios
Adjacent-channel interference (ACI) — overlapping channels (for example 1 and 3) Corrupted frames, retries Only 1, 6, 11 in 2.4 GHz; avoid overlapping bonded channels
Non-Wi-Fi devices in 2.4 GHz: microwave ovens (about 2.45 GHz), Bluetooth, cordless phones, baby monitors, wireless cameras Noise floor rises, SNR falls Move to 5/6 GHz; move or replace the device
Radar on 5 GHz DFS channels AP must leave the channel at once; clients briefly disconnected Avoid DFS channels near airports or weather radar
Physical obstacles — metal, concrete, water, lift shafts Absorption, reflection, dead spots Survey and place APs accordingly
Too much transmit power Large overlapping cells, more CCI, "sticky" clients Lower power, more APs
Too many SSIDs Each SSID sends beacons; airtime wasted Keep to about three or four SSIDs
Rogue or neighbour APs Extra CCI; possible security risk Rogue detection on the WLC
Hidden node — two clients cannot hear each other but both reach the AP Collisions at the AP Better AP placement; RTS/CTS

Symptoms users report: slow Wi-Fi despite "full bars" (good RSSI, poor SNR), frequent drops, low data rates and high retry counts. Tools: the client's RSSI and SNR readings, WLC dashboards and RRM reports, a spectrum analyzer to see non-Wi-Fi sources (Cisco CleanAir does this in the AP), and a site survey.

16.7 Packet Tracer Lab 16.1 — A secure two-AP WLAN with a channel plan (Beginner–Intermediate)

Objectives: 1.5.a, 1.5.c (1.5.b and 1.5.d as observation and discussion). New file. You will: build an ESS of two APs with one SSID on non-overlapping channels, secure it with WPA2-Personal (AES), connect laptops by DHCP, and test what happens with wrong security settings.

Packet Tracer limits: Packet Tracer shows association and a simple signal indicator, but it does not model RF interference, SNR or channel overlap realistically. Channel planning here is configuration practice; 16.3 and 16.6 explain the real-world effects.

In Figure 16.4 the wired part is at the top and the two radio cells below; where the cells overlap, a laptop can roam between APs without changing SSID or address.

Devices: 1 × ISR4331 (R-W), 1 × 2960 (SW-W), 2 × AccessPoint-PT (AP-A, AP-B), 2 × Laptop-PT (L1, L2).

AP-A Port 0 → SW-W Fa0/1 SSID Staff-WiFi, channel 1, WPA2-PSK, AES
AP-B Port 0 → SW-W Fa0/2 SSID Staff-WiFi, channel 6, WPA2-PSK, AES
L1, L2 Wireless WPC300N wireless card, DHCP

Step 2 — access points (Config tab → Port 1, the wireless port)

  1. AP-A: SSID Staff-WiFi, Channel 1, Authentication WPA2-PSK, pass phrase Lab16-Strong-Passphrase, Encryption AES.
  2. AP-B: identical, but Channel 6. Same SSID and security on both APs makes them one ESS, so laptops can roam.

Step 3 — laptops. Power off each laptop (Physical tab), remove the wired NIC module, insert WPC300N, power on. Then Desktop → PC Wireless → Connect: choose Staff-WiFi, enter the passphrase. (Alternatively Config → Wireless0: SSID, WPA2-PSK, key, DHCP.)

Reachability Laptop ping 192.168.30.1 Success
Leases R-W show ip dhcp binding Two wireless clients
Roaming Drag L1 from near AP-A to near AP-B Association line moves to AP-B; ping resumes

Experiments

  1. Wrong passphrase: reconnect L2 with one wrong character. It never associates, and gets no address.
  2. Security mismatch: set L2 to WPA2-PSK with TKIP instead of AES. Association fails: both sides must agree on the cipher.
  3. Personal versus Enterprise (optional; depends on your version): if AP-A offers WPA2 (Enterprise), add a Server-PT at 192.168.30.5 with the AAA service as a RADIUS server, enter the AP as a client with a shared secret, create user alice, and point AP-A at 192.168.30.5. Connect L1 as alice. If the option is missing, use Figure 16.3 instead.
  4. Channel discussion: change AP-B to channel 2. Packet Tracer still works — explain from Figure 16.1 why a real network would now suffer adjacent-channel interference, and set it back to 6.

Questions after the lab

  1. Why do both APs use the same SSID but different channels?
  2. Which channel would a third AP in between them use?
  3. Why is WPA2-PSK with a short passphrase weaker than WPA3-SAE with the same passphrase?

16.8 Chapter 16 summary notes (exam revision)

  • Terms: SSID (name), BSS (one AP radio + clients), BSSID (AP radio MAC), ESS (many APs, one SSID, roaming), IBSS (ad hoc). Wi-Fi is half duplex, CSMA/CA.
  • Standards: 11b/g 2.4 GHz; 11a 5 GHz; 11n Wi-Fi 4; 11ac Wi-Fi 5 (5 GHz); 11ax Wi-Fi 6/6E (6E adds 6 GHz); 11be Wi-Fi 7.
  • 2.4 GHz: longest range, crowded, only 1, 6, 11 non-overlapping. 5 GHz: many non-overlapping channels, DFS on some. 6 GHz: most channels, WPA3 required, new devices only.
  • Channel plan: neighbours on different non-overlapping channels; 20 MHz in 2.4 GHz; moderate power; RRM automates it on a WLC. CCI = same channel (sharing); ACI = overlapping channels (corruption).
  • RF: 0 dBm = 1 mW; +3 dB ×2; +10 dB ×10; RSSI −67 dBm for voice; SNR = signal − noise, ≥ 25 dB good. Absorption, reflection, refraction, scattering, diffraction, multipath. EIRP = Tx + gain − loss.
  • Security: WEP broken; WPA = TKIP (deprecated); WPA2 = AES-CCMP; WPA3 = SAE, PMF, GCMP-256 in 192-bit Enterprise. Personal = shared key; Enterprise = 802.1X/EAP + RADIUS (supplicant, authenticator, authentication server).
  • Interference: CCI, ACI, microwaves, Bluetooth, cordless phones, radar (DFS), obstacles, too much power, too many SSIDs.

Common conceptual mistakes

Mistake Correct idea
"−80 dBm is stronger than −60 dBm" Closer to zero is stronger: −60 dBm is 100 times stronger
"Full bars means good Wi-Fi" Bars show RSSI; poor SNR or congestion still makes it slow
"Hidden SSIDs are secure" The SSID still appears in client probes; use WPA2/WPA3
"More transmit power is always better" It enlarges cells, increases interference and mismatches clients
"Channels 1, 2 and 3 give three separate channels" They overlap heavily; use 1, 6, 11
